The plans for a dedicated nursery are on hold until we get our more traditional market gardens set up. In the meantime, we're documenting what we find in our {{< term "Zone 4" >}}Zone 4 consists of semi-wild managed woodlots and timber forage, while Zone 5 remains completely unmanaged wild habitat.{{< /term >}} areas of the property. Our plan is to eventually provide access to plant material for restoration projects and other food forest projects.

Native plants evolved alongside the pollinators, birds, fungi and soil life of the {{< term "Salish Sea bioregion" >}}The coastal and island ecosystem of southwestern BC and northwestern Washington, defined by its shared watershed and ecology rather than political borders.{{< /term >}}. Because of those long relationships, they tend to support far more of the local food web than imported ornamentals, and once established they generally need less water and care. We're not exclusively focused on native plants, but we'd be remiss to neglect these all-stars. 

## Plants we're excited about

**Oregon grape** is an evergreen shrub with holly-like leaves, yellow flowers in early spring — an important early food source for pollinators — and tart blue berries. We're selecting and propagating the best-tasting plants we can find.

**Salal** is a tough evergreen understory shrub with dark berries that can be eaten fresh or dried. It thrives in the shade of coastal forests. We're not the only ones to get excited about [bringing salal to more people](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/blueberries-superfoods-salal-berries-uvic-antioxidants-tannins-1.4565091). 

**Riparian species** that grow along streams and in wetlands, where their roots hold banks together and their shade keeps water cool for fish and other aquatic life. Watching and studying what we have already growing helps provide guidance and context for the wetland agriculture project.
