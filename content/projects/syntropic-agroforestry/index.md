A {{< term "syntropic agroforestry" >}}A farming system modelled on natural forest succession, using multi-layered plantings of trees, shrubs, and groundcovers to build soil fertility and produce food over time.{{< /term >}} system is planted to behave like a young forest. The approach was developed by Ernst Götsch, a Swiss farmer working in Brazil, who restored degraded land by imitating how forests regrow on their own.

## Growing in layers

Instead of rows of a single crop, plants are grouped by how much light they need and how tall they grow — a principle called {{< term "stratification" >}}Arranging plants in height layers — emergent, high, medium and low — so each captures light without crowding the others.{{< /term >}}. Tall canopy trees, fruiting shrubs, groundcovers and root crops share the same bed, each filling a different niche.

## Growing in time

Plants are also combined by lifespan. Fast pioneers such as annual vegetables and quick-growing support trees build soil and shelter first, while longer-lived fruit and nut trees establish beneath them. This mirrors {{< term "succession" >}}The natural sequence by which plant communities replace one another as soil, shade and shelter develop.{{< /term >}} in a wild forest.

## Pruning as fertiliser

Support plants are cut back regularly and the material is left on the ground — known as {{< term "chop-and-drop" >}}Cutting plant material and leaving it where it falls, as mulch and food for soil life.{{< /term >}}. This feeds soil life, holds moisture, and lets light back in for the plants that follow.

## Why it's an experiment here

Syntropic methods were refined in tropical and subtropical climates. Adapting them to the cool, wet winters and dry summers of the Sunshine Coast is new territory, which is why we treat these plots as a lab and record what works — and what doesn't.
